## 2. Modelo, preparación y lectura

Z, dos modos fermiónicos R,L por celda, Fock local de dimensión 4. La regla fija es

\[
 U=FJ,\qquad J=\prod_x e^{i\chi n_R(x)n_L(x)}.

de [1], PDF p.3, Ec.(1) y sección 3; orientación R/L escogida por su desplazamiento.

Para un entero M>=1,

\[
 f_s(x)=(2M)^{-1/2}{\bf1}_{[s,s+2M-1]}(x),\quad
 \Pi f(x)=(-1)^x f(x),\quad |0\rangle_s=f_s,\ |1\rangle_s=\Pi f_s.
\]

Son ortonormales: cada soporte tiene M sitios pares y M impares. En el código,
Pi es el Pauli X. Los estados |+>=(|0>+|1>)/sqrt(2) y
|->=(|0>-|1>)/sqrt(2) tienen soporte únicamente par e impar, respectivamente.

Cada paquete tiene componente fija. Paquetes con la misma componente tienen

## 3. Colisión exacta y entrelazamiento

Si R parte en x y L en y>x, coinciden en un sitio si y solo si y-x es par, en
t=(y-x)/2. Aplicamos J en t=0,...,T-1 antes de cada traslado. Una vez completo
el cruce de todos los componentes de los paquetes, cada trayectoria con paridad
igual ha recibido una fase e^{i chi}; las de paridad distinta ninguna.

Descontando F^T matemáticamente, el operador exacto es

\[
 S_\chi=e^{i\chi(1+\Pi_R\Pi_L)/2}
 =aI+b\Pi_R\Pi_L,\quad
 a=\frac{1+e^{i\chi}}2,\quad b=\frac{e^{i\chi}-1}2.
\]

El código es invariante bajo cada Pi, por lo que la fuga al terminar el cruce
es exactamente cero, para todo M. En orden lógico 00,01,10,11:

\[
 S_\chi=\begin{pmatrix}
 a&0&0&b\\0&a&b&0\\0&b&a&0\\b&0&0&a
 \end{pmatrix}
 =e^{i\chi/2}\exp\left(i\frac\chi2 X_R X_L\right).
\]

Sobre |00> da a|00>+b|11>. Los coeficientes de Schmidt son |a| y |b|;
la concurrencia es 2|ab|=|sin chi|. En chi=pi/2, el estado es
e^{i pi/4}(|00>+i|11>)/sqrt(2), con entrelazamiento máximo.
En chi=0 es identidad; en chi=pi es **-X_R X_L**, un producto local con fase
global, y no entrelaza. Ambos límites se verificaron.

Convención de salida: si se usan como modos finales f_{s+vT}, Pi f_{s+vT},
en lugar de trasladar cada modo inicial, aparece Z^T por paquete, porque
T_v Pi=-Pi T_v. La comprobación fermiónica incluye este factor. No confundir
un cambio de marco o una convención de lectura con un control físico adicional.

La ausencia de fuga se afirma **al completar el cruce**. Durante el encuentro
pueden correlacionarse paridad y forma espacial; la proyección sobre los mismos
cuatro modos no es una puerta unitaria. Un caso intermedio del verificador
(M=4,T=17,chi=pi/2) da fuga máxima 0.46875.

## 4. Composición y puertas de un qubit

Para n paquetes de componente fija, cada configuración de posiciones iniciales
sigue una trayectoria fija. Las fases de contacto de pares se multiplican.
Si E es el conjunto de pares R/L cuyos cruces se han completado (los demás aún
no han empezado),

\[
 S_E=\prod_{(i,j)\in E}\exp\left[i\chi(1+X_iX_j)/2\right].
\]

Todos los factores conmutan, incluso cuando comparten un paquete. El orden de
colisiones no genera un segundo eje lógico. La geometría real restringe además
E: paquetes de la misma componente no se cruzan y cada par opuesto se encuentra
a lo sumo una vez en la recta. No se afirma un enrutamiento arbitrario.

Prueba explícita: R en -8M, L_1 en 0, L_2 en 8M; todas las anchuras 2M.
En T=6M+1 ha terminado solo el primer cruce; en T=10M+1, ambos.
La evolución directa reproduce S_01 y S_02 S_01 para M=1,2,4,8 y los cuatro
acoplamientos del contrato. Los dos paquetes L son modos ortogonales de fermiones
idénticos: las etiquetas representan soportes disjuntos persistentes, no especies
distinguibles añadidas.

Un paquete aislado solo se traslada: en el marco natural produce Z^T, no una
rotación arbitraria. Una ancilla preparada en |+> o |->, eigenestado de X,
puede producir sobre el dato una rotación exp(+-i chi X/2), salvo fase global,
al cruzarlo. Este recurso también conserva el eje X. Preparar esa ancilla es
un recurso inicial concedido, no una prueba de preparación autónoma.

## 5. Obstrucción exacta a universalidad en este código

Primera vía: las colisiones conmutan con todos los X_i. Las posibles Z_i de
traslación solo invierten sus signos. Incluso concediendo **más** recursos que
los físicos —Z_i independientes, rotaciones X_i arbitrarias y colisiones XX
de cualquier ángulo—, cada unidad lógica satisface

\[
 V^\dagger X_i V=\pm X_i.
\]

En la base de paridad es una matriz monomial con permutaciones restringidas a
inversiones de bits; no puede mezclar coherentemente dos paridades de entrada.
Esta familia cerrada no es densa en U(2^n).

Testigo cuantitativo: H|+>=|0> tiene probabilidades de paridad (1/2,1/2),
mientras que cualquier operación admitida lleva el dato de paridad definida
a paridad definida. Su fidelidad con ese objetivo es como máximo 1/2 y la
distancia de traza (convención ||rho-sigma||_1/2) es al menos 1/sqrt(2).
La cota no disminuye al ensanchar los paquetes ni al añadir cruces.

Ancillas inicialmente independientes no reparan esto: para datos de paridad
definida, una unitaria diagonal en las paridades conjuntas deja fija la paridad
del dato, incluso si luego se descarta la ancilla. Las inversiones Z cambian
solo el signo. Se sigue la misma trayectoria de datos, sin sustituirla por una
ancilla preparada con la respuesta; no se concede una codificación nueva.

Segunda vía, microscópica: mu=0 hace de F una permutación fija de los modos
ocupados, y J es diagonal en sus ocupaciones. En coordenadas que siguen las
trayectorias, F^{-T}U^T es diagonal en las posiciones iniciales y conmuta con
cada Pi_i. Esta afirmación vale también en colisiones parciales, donde el código
puede tener fuga. Como el proyector del código conmuta con Pi_i, su compresión
también conmuta con X_i. Por tanto, esperar tiempos intermedios, permitir
deformaciones temporales y recuperar después el código no evita la obstrucción.

Esto completa el criterio de abandono declarado: **el código de nodos probado
no implementa un conjunto unitario universal con la evolución autónoma sin masa**.
No es un resultado sobre otra codificación no local, computación con medidas,
programas inicialmente entrelazados con los datos, ni masa no nula. La prueba
microscópica usa explícitamente mu=0; no se extrapola al modelo masivo.

## 6. Dos nodos y velocidad relativista: qué se obtiene

Con Fourier e^{ikx}, el paso libre empleado en el verificador es

\[
 W(k)=\operatorname{diag}(e^{-ik},e^{ik}),\quad W(\pi+q)=-W(q).
\]

Esta convención intercambia la orientación de componentes respecto a [1];
la dispersión y la colisión no cambian. Cerca de 0, W(q)=exp(-i q sigma_z).
Cerca de pi, retirar el factor de portador (-1)^t da la misma evolución.
En dos pasos se pliegan ambos nodos: W(pi+q)^2=W(q)^2. No se debe identificar
sin más cuasienergía pi/tau con una energía positiva infinita, ni afirmar que
se ha construido un vacío de baja energía de un Hamiltoniano único.

La concentración de los paquetes es controlable sin un ajuste numérico:

\[
 |\widehat f_s(k)|^2=\frac{\sin^2(Mk)}{4\pi M\sin^2(k/2)},\qquad
 \int_{|k|>\Lambda}|\widehat f_s(k)|^2dk
 \leq\min\left(1,\frac{1}{2M\sin^2(\Lambda/2)}\right).
\]

Pi desplaza esta distribución en pi. Para Lambda fijo en (0,pi/2), cada modo
se concentra en su nodo cuando M crece; por linealidad lo hace cualquier
superposición normalizada en un código de dimensión fija, alrededor de la
unión de nodos. Los modos no son estrictamente limitados en momento a M finito.
El programa incluye diagnósticos FFT, no los usa como prueba del límite.

Restaurando espacio x_phys=a x y tiempo t_phys=tau t, las envolventes libres
satisfacen el transporte de Dirac sin masa

\[
 (\partial_{t_{\rm phys}}+v\partial_{x_{\rm phys}})\phi_R=0,\quad
 (\partial_{t_{\rm phys}}-v\partial_{x_{\rm phys}})\phi_L=0,
 \qquad v=a/\tau.
\]

Son ecuaciones de las envolventes libres (mu=0), no una demostración de
covariancia Lorentz de toda la dinámica discreta interactuante. La fase de
colisión finita persiste al crecer M, pero no se han construido un vacío,
renormalización ni convergencia a una QFT interactuante relativista. La
obstrucción computacional ya cierra el candidato sin necesitar esa promoción.

def check(name, error, tol=TOL):
    error = float(error)
    CHECKS.append({"name": name, "error": error, "tolerance": tol,

def kron_all(factors):
    out = np.ones((1, 1), dtype=complex)
    for f in factors:
        out = np.kron(out, f)
    return out


def pauli(n, indices, op=X):
    return kron_all([op if i in indices else I for i in range(n)])


def gate(n, i, j, chi):
    z = np.exp(1j * chi)
    return (1 + z) / 2 * np.eye(2**n) + (z - 1) / 2 * pauli(n, (i, j))

    return kron_all([
        np.column_stack((np.ones(2*M), (-1.0)**(s + np.arange(2*M))))
        / np.sqrt(2*M) for s in starts
    ])

    coords = np.indices(shape)
    initial = [starts[i] + coords[i] for i in range(len(starts))]
    phase = np.ones(shape, dtype=complex)
    for t in range(steps):
        contacts = np.zeros(shape, dtype=int)
        for i, j in itertools.combinations(range(len(starts)), 2):
            if velocities[i] != velocities[j]:
                contacts += (initial[i] + velocities[i]*t ==
                             initial[j] + velocities[j]*t)
        phase *= np.exp(1j * chi * contacts)
    return phase.reshape(-1)


def projected_trajectory(starts, velocities, M, steps, chi):
    B = code_on_offsets(starts, M)
    check(f"gram:{starts}:{M}", np.linalg.norm(B.conj().T @ B - np.eye(B.shape[1])))
    evolved = trajectory_phase(starts, velocities, M, steps, chi)[:, None] * B
    K = B.conj().T @ evolved
    residual = np.linalg.norm(evolved - B @ K, ord=2)
    return K, residual**2


def two_packet_checks():
    rows = []
    for M in (1, 2, 8, 32):
        for chi in (0., .7, np.pi/2, np.pi):
            starts, steps = (-4*M, 4*M), 5*M+1
            K, leak = projected_trajectory(starts, (1, -1), M, steps, chi)
            S = gate(2, 0, 1, chi)
            err = np.linalg.norm(K - S, ord=2)
            check(f"two-packet-map:M={M}:chi={chi}", err)
            check(f"two-packet-leak:M={M}:chi={chi}", leak)
            check(f"two-packet-unitarity:M={M}:chi={chi}",
                  np.linalg.norm(K.conj().T @ K - np.eye(4)))
            v = K[:, 0]
            concurrence = 2*abs(v[0]*v[3]-v[1]*v[2])
            check(f"concurrence:M={M}:chi={chi}", abs(concurrence-abs(np.sin(chi))))
            rows.append(dict(M=M, chi=chi, steps=steps, map_error=err,
                             worst_leakage=leak, concurrence_00=concurrence))
    # Deliberately incomplete crossing: closure need not hold mid-collision.
    partial, leak = projected_trajectory((-16, 16), (1, -1), 4, 17, np.pi/2)
    check("partial-crossing-is-not-a-closed-gate", 0. if leak > .01 else 1.)
    for i in (0, 1):
        Xi = pauli(2, (i,))
        check(f"partial-crossing-still-preserves-parity:{i}", np.linalg.norm(partial@Xi-Xi@partial))
    return rows, dict(M=4, steps=17, chi=np.pi/2, worst_leakage=leak)


def antisymmetric_lattice_checks():
    """Full two-fermion amplitudes, actual spatial motion; no trajectory formula."""
    rows = []
    L = 64
    modes = np.arange(2*L)
    dest = 2*((modes//2 + np.where(modes % 2 == 0, 1, -1)) % L) + modes % 2
    contact = ((modes[:, None]//2 == modes[None, :]//2) &
               (modes[:, None] % 2 != modes[None, :] % 2))

    def basis(M, starts):
        orbitals = []
        for spin, s in enumerate(starts):
            pair = []
            sites = np.arange(s, s+2*M)
            for node in (0, 1):
                v = np.zeros(2*L, dtype=complex)
                v[2*sites+spin] = (-1.0)**(node*sites) / np.sqrt(2*M)
                pair.append(v)
            orbitals.append(pair)
        return np.array([(np.outer(r, l)-np.outer(l, r))/np.sqrt(2)
                         for r in orbitals[0] for l in orbitals[1]])

    for M in (1, 2, 3):
        starts, steps = (8, 8+6*M), 4*M+1
        # All trajectories stay away from the periodic boundary.
        assert starts[0] + steps + 2*M < L
        assert starts[1] - steps >= 0
        initial = basis(M, starts)
        output = basis(M, (starts[0]+steps, starts[1]-steps))
        for chi in (0., .7, np.pi/2, np.pi):
            evolved = initial.copy()
            phase = np.where(contact, np.exp(1j*chi), 1)
            for _ in range(steps):
                moved = np.zeros_like(evolved)
                moved[:, dest[:, None], dest[None, :]] = evolved * phase
                evolved = moved
            # Natural moving-envelope basis uses Pi AFTER the translation:
            # each node-1 state acquires (-1)^steps.
            K = output.reshape(4, -1).conj() @ evolved.reshape(4, -1).T
            expected = (pauli(2, (0, 1), Z) if steps % 2 else np.eye(4)) @ gate(2, 0, 1, chi)
            err = np.linalg.norm(K-expected, ord=2)
            reconstructed = (output.reshape(4, -1).T @ K).T.reshape(evolved.shape)
            leak = np.linalg.norm((evolved-reconstructed).reshape(4, -1), ord=2)**2
            anti = np.linalg.norm(evolved + evolved.transpose(0, 2, 1))
            check(f"fermionic-map:M={M}:chi={chi}", err)
            check(f"fermionic-leak:M={M}:chi={chi}", leak)
            check(f"fermionic-antisymmetry:M={M}:chi={chi}", anti)
            rows.append(dict(M=M, L=L, steps=steps, chi=chi,
                             map_error=err, worst_leakage=leak, antisymmetry_error=anti))
    return rows


def composition_checks():
    rows = []
    for M in (1, 2, 4, 8):
        for chi in (0., .7, np.pi/2, np.pi):
            starts = (-8*M, 0, 8*M)
            for steps, edges in ((6*M+1, [(0, 1)]), (10*M+1, [(0, 1), (0, 2)])):
                K, leak = projected_trajectory(starts, (1, -1, -1), M, steps, chi)
                expected = np.eye(8, dtype=complex)
                for i, j in edges:
                    expected = gate(3, i, j, chi) @ expected
                err = np.linalg.norm(K-expected, ord=2)
                check(f"three-packet-composition:M={M}:chi={chi}:t={steps}", err)
                check(f"three-packet-leak:M={M}:chi={chi}:t={steps}", leak)
                rows.append(dict(M=M, chi=chi, steps=steps, edges=edges,
                                 map_error=err, worst_leakage=leak))
    return rows


def obstruction_checks():
    # Intentionally larger than the physical gate set: independent Z flips,
    # arbitrary XX collision angles and arbitrary local X rotations. Still
    # monomial in the X basis, so failure here covers the smaller actual set.
    n = 3
    B = kron_all([H]*n)
    U = np.eye(2**n, dtype=complex)
    for i, j, angle in ((0, 1, .7), (1, 2, 1.1), (0, 2, .4)):
        U = gate(n, i, j, angle) @ pauli(n, (i,), Z) @ U
        U = (np.cos(.37)*np.eye(2**n)+1j*np.sin(.37)*pauli(n, (j,))) @ U
    in_parity_basis = B.conj().T @ U @ B
    absolute = np.abs(in_parity_basis)
    check("overgrant-monomial-one-entry-per-column", np.max(abs(absolute.max(axis=0)-1)))
    check("overgrant-monomial-one-entry-per-row", np.max(abs(absolute.max(axis=1)-1)))
    check("overgrant-monomial-other-entries-zero", np.max(np.minimum(absolute, abs(absolute-1))))

    # |+>_X is a definite parity state. H sends it to |0>_node,
    # with parity probabilities (1/2,1/2), impossible for a monomial unitary.
    plus = np.array([1., 1.]) / np.sqrt(2)
    target = H @ plus
    fidelity = abs(np.vdot(plus, target))**2
    minus = np.array([1., -1.]) / np.sqrt(2)
    check("H-witness-both-parities-fidelity-one-half", max(abs(fidelity-.5),
          abs(abs(np.vdot(minus, target))**2-.5)))
    # An arbitrary ancilla state and a diagonal-in-X interaction cannot turn
    # a definite data parity into a superposition, even on discarding ancilla.
    ancilla = np.array([np.sqrt(.3), np.exp(.31j)*np.sqrt(.7)])
    state = gate(2, 0, 1, .7) @ np.kron(plus, ancilla)
    matrix = state.reshape(2, 2)
    reduced = matrix @ matrix.conj().T
    check("ancilla-preserves-definite-data-parity",
          np.linalg.norm(reduced-np.outer(plus, plus)))
    check("chi-pi-is-minus-product-XX", np.linalg.norm(gate(2, 0, 1, np.pi)+np.kron(X, X)))
    return dict(H_witness_max_fidelity=float(fidelity),
                H_witness_min_trace_distance=float(np.sqrt(1-fidelity)),
                overgrant_monomial_error=float(np.max(np.minimum(absolute, abs(absolute-1)))))


def physical_checks():
    # Fourier convention e^{ikx}, actual right shift has eigenvalue e^{-ik}.
    q = np.linspace(-.2, .2, 81)
    dispersion_error = 0.
    for k in q:
        W = np.diag([np.exp(-1j*k), np.exp(1j*k)])
        Wpi = np.diag([np.exp(-1j*(k+np.pi)), np.exp(1j*(k+np.pi))])
        dispersion_error = max(dispersion_error, np.linalg.norm(Wpi+W))
    check("two-node-quasienergy-shift", dispersion_error)
    # Finite-ring quadrature of infinite-line packet Fourier density; these
    # numbers are diagnostics, not certified integrals. Bound in report is exact.
    N, cutoff = 65536, .3
    k = 2*np.pi*np.fft.fftfreq(N)
    rows = []
    for M in (1, 2, 8, 32, 128):
        f = np.zeros(N)
        f[:2*M] = 1/np.sqrt(2*M)
        g = f * (-1.0)**np.arange(N)
        probability = abs(np.fft.fft(f, norm="ortho"))**2
        probability_pi = abs(np.fft.fft(g, norm="ortho"))**2
        check(f"Fourier-node-shift:M={M}",
              np.linalg.norm(probability_pi-np.roll(probability, N//2)))
        tail = float(probability[abs(k)>cutoff].sum())
        bound = min(1., 1/(2*M*np.sin(cutoff/2)**2))
        rows.append(dict(M=M, fft_points=N, cutoff=cutoff,
                         finite_ring_tail_probability=tail, infinite_line_tail_upper_bound=float(bound)))
    return dict(node_dispersion_error=float(dispersion_error), packet_tails=rows)

                                            1. Introduction
                                                 Quantum walks (QWs) describe the evolution of one-particle quantum states on a lattice, or
                                            more generally, on a graph. The quantum walk evolution is linear in the quantum state and the
                                            quantum aspect of the evolution occurs in the interference between the different paths available to
                                            the walker. There are two kinds of quantum walks: continuous time QWs, where the evolution
                                            operator of the system given in terms of an Hamiltonian can be applied at any time (see Farhi et al. [2]),
                                            and discrete-time QWs, where the evolution operator is applied in discrete unitary time-steps. The
                                            discrete-time model, which appeared already in the Feynman discretization of the Dirac equation [3],
                                            was later rediscovered in quantum information [4–8], and proved to be a versatile platform for various
                                            scopes. For example, QWs have been used for empowering quantum algorithms, such as database
                                            search [9,10], or graph isomorphism [11,12]. Moreover, quantum walks have been studied as a
                                            simulation tool for relativistic quantum fields [13–28], and they have been used as discrete models of
                                            spacetime [29–32].
                                                 QWs are among the most promising quantum simulators with possible realizations in a variety of
                                            physical systems, such as nuclear magnetic resonance [33,34], trapped ions [35], integrated photonics,
                                            and bulk optics [36–39].

        New research perspectives are unfolding in the scenario of multi-particle interacting quantum
walks where two or more walking particles are coupled via non-linear (in the field) unitary
operators. The properties of these systems are still largely unexplored. Both continuous-time [40]
and discrete-time [41] quantum walks on sparse unweighted graphs are equivalent in power to
the quantum circuit model. However, it is highly non trivial to design a suitable architecture for
universal quantum computation based on quantum walks. Within this perspective a possible route has
been suggested in Ref. [42] based on interacting multi-particle quantum walks with indistinguishable
particles (Bosons or Fermions), proving that “almost any interaction” is universal. Among the universal
interacting many-body systems are the models with coupling term of the form χδx1 ,x2 n̂( x1 )n̂( x2 ), with
n̂( x ) the number operator at site x. The latter two-body interaction lies at the basis of notable integrable
quantum systems in one space dimension such as the Hubbard and the Thirring Hamiltonian models.
        The first attempt at the analysis of interacting quantum walks was carried out in Ref. [43]. More
recently, in Ref. [1], the authors proposed a discrete-time analogue of the Thirring model which is
indeed a Fermionic quantum cellular automaton, whose dynamics in the two-particles sector reduces
to an interacting two-particle quantum walk. As for its Hamiltonian counterpart, the discrete-time
interacting walk has been solved analytically in the case of two Fermions. Analogously to any
Hamiltonian integrable system, also in the discrete-time case the solution is based on the Bethe Ansatz
technique. However, discreteness of the evolution prevents the application of the usual Ansatz, and a
new Ansatz has been introduced successfully [1].
        In this paper we present an original simplified derivation of the solution of Ref. [1] which exploits
the symmetries of the interacting walk. We present the diagonalization of the evolution operator and
the characterization of its spectrum. We explicitly write the two particle states corresponding to the
scattering solutions of the system, having eigenvalues in the continuous spectrum of the evolution
operator. We then show how the present model predicts the formation of bound states, which are
eigenstates of the interacting walk corresponding to the discrete spectrum. We provide also in this
case the analytic expression of such molecular states.
        We remark the phenomenological differences between the Hamiltonian model and the
discrete-time one. First we see that the set of possible scattering solutions is larger in the discrete-time
case: for a fixed value total momentum, a non trivial transfer of relative momentum can occur besides
the simple exchange of momentum between the two particles, differently from the Hamiltonian case.
Also the family of bound states appearing in the discrete-time scenario is larger than the corresponding
Hamiltonian one. Indeed, for any fixed value of the coupling constant, a bound state exists with
any possible value of the total momentum, while for Hamiltonian systems bound states cannot have
arbitrary total momentum.
        Finally we show that in the set of solutions for the interacting walk there are perfectly localized
states (namely states which lie on a finite number of lattice sites) and, differently from the Hamiltonian
systems, bound states exist also for vanishing coupling constant. In addition to the exact analytical
solution of the dynamics we show the simulation of some significant initial state.

4. Symmetries of the Thirring Quantum Walk
      The Thirring walk manifests some symmetries that allow to simplify the derivation and the
study of the solutions. First of all, as we already mentioned, one can show that the interaction V (χ)
commutes with the total number operator. This means that one can study the walk dynamics separately
for each fixed number of particles. We focus here on the two-particle walk U2 = W2 V2 (χ), where
W2 = W ⊗ W and V2 (χ) = eiχδy,0 (1−δa1 ,a2 ) .
      Since the interacting walk U2 commutes with the translations in the centre of mass coordinate w,

the interacting walk of two particles can be expressed in terms of a one-dimensional QW U2 (χ, p) =

5.4. Solutions for p ∈ {0, π/2}
    The solutions that we presented in the previous discussion do not cover the extreme values
p = 0, π/2 (see Ref. [1] for a reference). Let us consider for definiteness the case p = 0 since the other
Version April 24, 2018 submitted to Entropy                                                              13 of 16



case is obtained in a similar way. For e−iω 6= 1 the previous analysis still holds. Indeed, noticing
that ω±± (0, k) = ±2ω (k ), we have ω (k) ∈ R and ω (k ) 6= 0 if and only if k ∈ Γ f ∪ Γ0 ∪ Γ2 , whereas
ω±∓ (0, k ) = 0 for all k ∈ C. This means that the solutions |ψk+ i of Eq. (18) are actually eigenvectors of
U2 (χ, 0). Thus, the spectrum is made by a continuous part, given by the arc of the unit circle containing
−1 and having e±2iω (0) as extremes, and a point spectrum with two points: e−2iω (k̃) , where k̃ is the
solution of T+ = 0 for p = 0, and 1. As shown in Ref. [1], 1 is a separated part of the spectrum of
U2 (χ, 0) and the corresponding eigenspace is a separable Hilbert space of stationary bound states.
This fact underlines an important feature of the Thirring walk not shared by analogous Hamiltonian
models. It is remarkable that this behaviour occurs also for the free walk with χ = 0. In Fig. 6 we show
the probability distribution of two states having he properties hereby discussed. It is worth noticing
that all the states v+−
                      k   with k ∈ (−π, π ] are eigenvectors relative to the eigenvalue 1, and thus they
generate a subspace on which the walk acts identically. We remark that this behaviour relies on the
fact that the dispersion relation in one dimension is an even function of k.
      In Fig. 4 is depicted the discrete spectrum of the interacting walk together with the continuous
spectrum as a function of the total momentum p. The solid curves in the gaps between the
continuous bands denote the discrete spectrum for different values of the coupling constant χ =
2π/3, 3π/7, −3π/7, −2π/3. Molecule states appear also in the Hadamard walk with the same
on-site interaction [48].

6. Conclusions
     In this work we reviewed the Thirring quantum walk providing a simplified derivation of its
solutions for Fermionic particles. The simplified derivation relies on the symmetric properties of the
walk evolution operator allowing to separate the subspace of solutions affected by the interaction
from the subspace where the interaction step acts trivially. The interaction term is the most general
number-preserving interaction in one dimension, whereas the free evolution is provided by the Dirac
QW [44].
     We showed the explicit derivation of the scattering solutions (solutions for the continuous
spectrum) as well as for the bound-state solutions. The Thirring walk features also localized bound
states (namely, states whose support is finite on the lattice) when e−iω = e±i2p . Such solutions exist
only when the coupling constant is χ = 2p. In Fig. 3 is depicted the evolution of a perfectly localized
state showing the overlapping with bound states components. In Fig. 5 we reported the evolution
of a bound state of the two particles peaked around a certain value of the total momentum: one
can appreciate that the probability distribution remains localized on the main diagonal during the
evolution.
     Finally, we discussed also the class of proper eigenvectors arising in the free theory highlighting
another difference between the discrete model of the present work with analogous Hamiltonian
models.
