## 2. What follows from the printed formulas

Elementary derivations by the author, by hand, **not checked by any
script**. Convention as in the Weyl/Dirac verifier: `T_a := i ∂_{p_a} W`
at `p = 0`. (The overall sign of `T_a` depends on the momentum sign
convention and does not affect any clause below.)

**(5.12).**
(i) `W(0) = I`, scalar.
(ii)–(iii) Each factor is `cos p_a + i sin p_a σ_a`, so `∂_a W(0) = i σ_a`
and `T_a = −σ_a`: Hermitian and traceless.
(iv) `{T_a, T_b} = 2 δ_ab I`, hence `c_0 = 1` in CJWW units (unit lattice
spacing, one update per time unit), consistent with Eq. (5.14).
(v) (iv) alone gives `A(n)^2 = |n|^2 I` for `A(n) = n_a T_a`, the
equivalent form declared in contract Sec. 2.4; tracelessness (iii) is what
excludes an identity component (tilt) in the linear term.

**(5.15).**
Unitarity for real `p` (checked by hand): the rows have norm
`cos^2 p_1 cos^2 p_2 + cos^2 p_1 sin^2 p_2 + sin^2 p_1 = 1` and are
orthogonal.
(i) `W(0) = I`.
(ii)–(iii) `∂_1 W(0) = i σ_1`, `∂_2 W(0) = −i σ_2`, `∂_3 W(0) = i σ_3`, so
`T = (−σ_1, σ_2, −σ_3)`: Hermitian and traceless.
(iv) The three anticommute and square to `I`: `{T_a, T_b} = 2 δ_ab I`,
`c_0 = 1`.
(v) As for (5.12): `A(n)^2 = |n|^2 I` from (iv), no identity component
from (iii).

**Support and units.** In both walks the composite support contains
`(±1, ±1, ±1)` (for (5.12), the product of three factors each with steps

l. 147. The pair itself is still taken from that single source; the

                              5.4. 3D cones and rate functions
   Some quantum walks on the three-dimensional cubic lattice were proposed by Bialinycki-Birula [6]
as discrete approximations to the Weyl, Dirac, and Maxwell equations. The interest in that paper
is mainly in the continuum limit, and the possible variations of nearest neighbour walks automata
for which this works. But one can take the discrete model as a system in its own right and analyze
its propagation properties. Let us consider the simplest of these, the “Weyl equation” model in [6].
We then have K = C2 , and
                                 W (p1 , p2 , p3 ) = eip1 σ1 eip2 σ2 eip3 σ3 .                 (5.12)

This gives
                                                                                    
                ω± (p) = ±ω(p) = ± arccos cos p1 cos p2 cos p3 − sin p1 sin p2 sin p3 .        (5.13)

A characteristic feature here are conical singularities at p = 0 and eight inequivalent points in the
Brillouin zone, where p1 , p2 , p3 ∈ πZ, meaning that all cos pi = ±1 and each matrix factor in (5.12)
is ±1. Expanding the cosine of (5.13) to second order in a small deviation p from such a conical
point p∨ , we get, for any combination of the signs cos(p∨ k ),

                                ω(p∨ + p)2 = (p21 + p21 + p21 ) + O(p3 )                       (5.14)

This is in keeping with the remarks in Section 2.2 on regular vs. singular points in a dispersion
relation. Along a straight line through p∨ we can use one-parameter perturbation theory, in which
we can always choose analytic branches, corresponding to the straight lines in the boundary of the
cone, but as a function of three parameters ω(p + p∨ ) ≈ ω(p∨ ) ± |p| is not analytic. In the limit
taken in [6] only small momenta survive, so the propagation is indeed exactly the familiar light cone
from relativistic physics. However, in this model faster propagation speeds can arise from points at
a finite distance from the singularities. The overall propagation region is shown in Figure 5.
